\Title{Broadband RF Design with MEEP: A Set of 3D-Printed Open-Source RF Horns in the multi-GHz Bandwidth}
\TitleCitation{Broadband RF Design with MEEP: A Set of 3D-Printed Open-Source RF Horns in the multi-GHz Bandwidth}

\abstract{Radio-frequency (RF) antenna design and fabrication traditionally proceeds in three phases.  First, the design performance is modeled with expensive, proprietary software.  Second, the design is built using precision metal machining or printed circuit board (PCB) fabrication.  Third, the result is characterized using RF measurement tools.  The process can include machine learning for optimization.  We have developed an open-source alternative process that utilizes the MIT Electromagnetic Equation Propagator (MEEP) package for design and simulation, and open-source 3D printing with conductive polymer for fabrication.  Using this process, we created a set of broadband RF horn antennas.  The radiation patterns and the VSWR match our MEEP calculations, while the cross-polarization ratio matches theoretical expectations.  These results indicate that our creations are linearly polarized, broadband RF horn antennas with bandwidth [4.0-18.0] GHz, or a fractional bandwidth of 127\%.  Because the maximum frequency of our network analyzer is 18 GHz, these results are lower limits.  Our results therefore represent a successful broadband open-source CEM/CAD workflow utilizing direct, repeatable conductive printing that circumvents the need for electroplating processes.}

\keyword{Computational Electromagnetism (CEM), Additive Manufacturing, MEEP, \\ RF Engineering, Open-Source Design}

\section{Introduction}

Broadband RF antennas are ubiquitous tools within scientific instrumentation and communications.  Traditionally, these are designed with expensive, proprietary software packages \cite{hfss,xfdtd}.  Designs are fabricated by cutting metal with precision machine tools (subtractive manufacturing).  Compared to additive manufacturing techniques, subtractive manufacturing can be costly for rapid prototyping \cite{segoviaguerrero2025additive-29b,add_vs_sub}.  Open-source additive manufacturing boosts cost efficiency in both the design and fabrication, provided the 3D printing filament has sufficient conductivity.

Development of new RF antenna designs requires exploration of conductor shapes that set the boundary conditions for radiated signals.  Developing new shapes that meet design requirements has been aided by machine learning (ML) strategies \cite{gajbhiye2025comprehensive-fdb,goudos2016evolutionary-cc7,linden1999evolving-8c2}.  While mixing new ML algorithms into open CEM code is straightforward, incorporating them into proprietary software is often challenging.  Ideally, RF engineers would keep the  functionality of proprietary software, while retaining the flexibility to incorporate new ML techniques.  Creating a design process based on open CEM tools achieves this goal.

Previously, J. C. Hanson (2021) demonstrated that the open-source MEEP software package may be used as an RF antenna design tool \cite{hanson2021broadband-cab}.  MEEP operates via the FDTD algorithm for Maxwell's Equations on a Yee lattice \cite{10.1016/j.cpc.2009.11.008}.  The key to the technique is that MEEP is written to account for the scale-invariance of Maxwell's Equations.  MEEP is normally used for designs with wavelengths at the $\mu$m scale.  Designs with cm wavelengths may be created by reinterpreting length units in MEEP.  Using this approach, J. C. Hanson produced RF phased array designs in two and three dimensions using MEEP functionality \cite{hanson2021broadband-cab}.  The designs included broadband RF horn elements in the [0.5-5] GHz bandwidth.  Further, J. C. Hanson demonstrated that open-source CAD software may be used to create complex designs that can be 3D printed \cite{meepcon_hanson}.

In recent years, RF antenna systems have begun to be fabricated with 3D printing.  The authors of \cite{alkaraki2020mmwave-bcd} presented a hybrid strategy that combined traditional PCB fabrication, non-conductive 3D printing, and metallization of 3D printed structures.  The authors of \cite{yoo2022hollow-9a5} produced a waveguide-fed holographic beam-forming antenna using a dual print-head 3D printing system that combined non-conductive PLA supports with conductive filament.  The result was a steerable beam at frequencies near 10 GHz.  The authors of \cite{yurduseven2017computational-82f} produced a Mills-Cross, frequency-diverse, metasurface antenna capable of electronic steering in the K-band, using a mixture of non-conductive PLA and conductive filament.  Finally, the authors of \cite{alkaraki2025lowcost-d32} fabricated RF horns with a 123.5\% fractional bandwidth after coating 3D printed polymer with silver paint.

In this work, we present the first open-source, broadband, 3D printed set of RF horns, designed with MEEP and fabricated entirely from conductive 3D-printer filament.  Note that the authors are MEEP users, but not MEEP developers.  Where appropriate, we have cited the creators of MEEP \cite{10.1016/j.cpc.2009.11.008}.  In Sec. \ref{sec:mat_meth}, we explain the parametric design, CAD, and fabrication techniques.  In Sec. \ref{sec:results}, we show that the $\mathbf{E}$-plane and $\mathbf{H}$-plane radiation patterns, the VSWR, and the cross-polarization ratios match predictions.  In Sec. \ref{sec:conc}, we discuss the limitations of our analysis, summarize key results, and discuss applications and future directions for this research.

\section{Methods and Materials}
\label{sec:mat_meth}

The goal of this work was to use open CEM tools to design, model, and fabricate linearly polarized RF horn antennas in the multi-GHz range via additive manufacturing.  Figure \ref{fig:1} contains a diagram of the design and fabrication process.  Using parametric CAD design, we can specify antenna shapes using analytic functions that are then translated into the open CAD design tool kLayout.  Antenna designs created with kLayout can be imported into MEEP via the GDSII file format.  GDSII files can also be translated into 3D printer files, ensuring that the device and model match.  In Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left), the CAD design in kLayout is shown.  In Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (middle) and (right), the model is rendered using Mayavi.

Our open fabrication technique hinges on the existence of commercially available conductive 3D printer filament.  Multi3D\footnote{See \url{https://www.multi3dmaterials.com}} has shown that a copper-doped thermoplastic serves as a 3D printer filament with resistivities of $\approx 10^{-2}$ $\Omega$ cm, depending on the printing pattern.  Using a tungsten-tipped 3D printing head and Electrifi filament from Multi3D, we can complete antenna prints with volumes of $\approx 350$ cm$^3$ in several hours.  The printed structures are fitted with RF connectors and tested as receivers on custom mounts that swivel in zenith and azimuthal angles.  A network analyzer with bandwidth [0-18] GHz was used to measure complex S11 and S21 parameters, with a calibrated log-periodic dipole array (LPDA) antenna as the transmitter.

\subsection{CEM Design and Open-Source CAD}
\label{sec:mat_meth_1}

The open-source CEM design begins with choosing parametric design parameters for the exponential RF horn and importing them into kLayout (Fig. \ref{fig:1}-\ref{fig:1a}).  Let the origin refer to the center of the left edge of the cavity in Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left).  The horn cavity is the rectangular volume where the coaxial cable attaches, with xy-dimensions $c_l \times c_w$  (Tab. \ref{tab:1}).  The horn surfaces are the curved structures that connect the cavity to open space, with overall length $s_l$ in the x-direction, including the cavity.  The opening is the area where the surfaces stop and radiation exits the horn.  The opening has width $w$ and height $z$ in the y and z-directions, respectively.  Using these variables, Equations \ref{eq:1} and \ref{eq:2} specify the shape of the RF horn surfaces in the $xy$-plane:

\begin{align}
f(x) &= \frac{c_w}{2}\exp(k(x-c_l)) \label{eq:1} \\
k &= (s_l-c_l)^{-1} \ln\left(\frac{w}{c_w}\right) \label{eq:2}
\end{align}

\noindent
The function $f(x)$ in Equation \ref{eq:1} describes the upper surface in Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left), while $-f(x)$ describes the lower surface.  Table \ref{tab:1} contains the values for $c_l$, $c_w$, $s_l$, and $w$.  The RF horn is designed to be linearly polarized in the $y$-direction.  The RF horn design has a height $z$, out of the page in Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left), and each conductive and dielectric surface, including the coaxial cable, has a specified thickness in the xy-plane and z-direction.

In previous studies \cite{hanson2021broadband-cab}, antenna structures were instantiated directly as native MEEP objects.  In the present workflow, the antenna geometry is instead generated parametrically and transferred to kLayout, where it can be inspected and exported in GDSII format.  The same geometry is then used for the MEEP calculation and for conversion to a printable 3D model.  This approach preserves the flexibility of a scripted parametric design while avoiding the need to reproduce a complex antenna from many simple MEEP primitives.  The implementation code used to generate and simulate the antenna is maintained in the open-source project repository cited in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsubsection{VSWR Calculations}

The voltage standing wave ratio (VSWR) is expressed in terms of the magnitude of the complex reflection coefficient $\Gamma$ at the antenna input port \cite{pozar}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{VSWR}=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}.
\label{eq:3}
\end{equation}
In the MEEP model, a broadband excitation is launched through the modeled coaxial feed.  A MEEP \texttt{FluxMonitor} is used to separate the incident and reflected contributions at the input line, from which $\Gamma$ and the corresponding VSWR are calculated\footnote{See \url{https://meep.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Python_Tutorials/GDS_Import}.}.  The model is fully three-dimensional and uses the material properties of the Electrifi filament.  Implementation details and source code are provided in the open-source repository cited in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsubsection{Radiation Pattern Calculations}

We compute the antenna radiation pattern in MEEP using near-to-far field projection\footnote{See \url{https://meep.readthedocs.io/en/master/Python_Tutorials/Near_to_Far_Field_Spectra}.}.  A \verb+NearToFarRegion+ encloses the radiating structure, and the \verb+meep.get_farfield()+ routine is used to evaluate the complex electric and magnetic fields in the far field.  In the coordinate system of Fig. \ref{fig:1a}, the horn boresight is the $+x$ direction, the $xy$-plane is the $\mathbf{E}$-plane, and the $xz$-plane is the $\mathbf{H}$-plane.

The time-averaged Poynting vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}^{*}\right),
\end{equation}
and the radiated power used for the angular pattern is the component projected along the radial direction,
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm r}=\mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}.
\end{equation}
For the $\mathbf{E}$-plane, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)$, while for the $\mathbf{H}$-plane, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)$.  The normalized radiation pattern is reported in decibels as
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm norm}=10\log_{10}\left[\frac{P_{\rm r}}{\max(P_{\rm r})}\right] \label{eq:Pnorm}
\end{equation}
In Eq. \ref{eq:Pnorm}, $P_{\rm norm}$ and $P_{\rm r}$ are functions of $\phi$ and $\theta$ when calculating the E and H-planes, respectively. The corresponding source code is maintained in the open-source repository cited in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsection{Fabrication Technique}
\label{sec:mat_meth_2}

The parametric horn geometry was converted from the kLayout/GDSII design into a printable 3D model and fine-tuned for fabrication.  The antennas were printed directly from Multi3D Electrifi conductive filament on a Prusa XL equipped with a tungsten-tipped nozzle.  Electrifi was printed at approximately 135--145$^{\circ}$C without bed heating.  Because the horn is tall and narrow, masking tape with adhesive and removable buttresses were used to improve build-plate adhesion and mechanical stability during printing.  After printing, the temporary supports were removed and an SMA connector was attached to the cavity so that the center conductor extended into the feed region.  No secondary electroplating or conductive coating was applied to the radiating structure.  One example of the RF horns is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:3}, with a 12-inch ruler for scale.

\subsection{VSWR Results}
\label{sec:vswr}

The 3D-printed RF horn was mounted to a 1.0 meter tripod with a set screw, and aimed vertically. Each antenna was connected to port~1 of an Agilent Technologies E5063A network analyzer [100~kHz--18~GHz] through a 6~ft Coleman Cable 8421F M17/75-RG214 N-type coaxial cable and an SMA-to-N adapter. The network analyzer was calibrated over the full measurement bandwidth using a calibration kit consisting of short, open, and $50~\Omega$ load standards.  The calibration reference plane was between the RF horns and the RG214 cable so that cable losses were removed from the S11 measurement. S11 data was averaged using 999 traces per average with 2.5\% smoothing.  The calibrated S11 trace was recorded separately for each horn.  The VSWR was calculated using Eq. \ref{eq:3}, with $\Gamma=S_{11}$. 

Our MEEP/CAD matched the dimensions of the RF horns.  For the \verb+mp.Medium+ that specified material properties of the Electrifi, resistivities of 0.0035 $\Omega$ cm and 0.0060 $\Omega$ cm were assumed for HORN1 and HORN2-HORN8, respectively.  These numbers were taken from the technical specifications for the batches of Electrifi corresponding to the fabricated horns.  The dielectric portion of the coaxial cable was modeled in MEEP with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r=2.0$ and a loss tangent of $8\times 10^{-3}$ at 6 GHz.  All other surfaces cable components were modeled using \texttt{mp.metal} with ideal conductivity.

The simulated $\Gamma$ was calculated from two runs.  The simulation was first run with a cylindrical coaxial cable object that extended into an outer perfectly-matched layer (PML) 5 MEEP units thick.  The thick PML ensured the first run had no reflections.  The pulse passed a \texttt{FluxMonitor} MEEP object set to record flux versus frequency.  The simulation was then reset, and the RF horn object was added to the cable.  The simulation was run a second time, with $-1$ times original flux pre-loaded to the \texttt{FluxMonitor}.  The negative pre-loading step ensured that the \texttt{FluxMonitor} did not record incident flux twice.  Finally, $\Gamma$ was computed by taking the square-root of the ratio of reflected flux from the second run to the incident flux in the first run.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{revised_vswr_Nov19.pdf}
\caption{\label{fig:vswr_lab} Dashed line: HORN1 calibrated VSWR with $\sigma = 28,600$ S m$^{-1}$.  Solid line: HORN2-HORN8 average calibrated VSWR with $\sigma = 16,700$ S m$^{-1}$.  Dot-dashed line: MEEP simulation.}
\end{figure}

The calibrated VSWR data is shown with the calculated VSWR from MEEP in Fig. \ref{fig:vswr_lab}.  The VSWR is inversely proportional to frequency and begins to match MEEP simulation output as frequency increases.  The MEEP results do not shift appreciably when we toggle between the Electrifi resistivities of 0.0035 $\Omega$ cm and 0.0060 $\Omega$ cm.  For the fabricated horns, we observed clear S21 transmission above the noise floor starting at 5.5 GHz.  The RF signal at 5.5 GHz, however, radiated inefficiently (see Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity}).  

Though HORN1 was printed with Electrifi that was more conductive than HORN2-HORN8, after calibrating for losses in RF cables, the VSWR of HORN1 was larger than that of HORN2-HORN8.  Also, conductive silver paste was added between the SMA bulkhead connector of HORN1, while HORN2-HORN8 do not have the paste. There are two plausible hypotheses for the differences in Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity}.  First, the horns with lower conductivity might display both a smaller $\Gamma$ and lower radiation efficiency because they are absorbing RF power.  Second, the SMA connnections for HORN2-HORN8 might actually be better matched, leading to lower VSWR.

The fractional bandwidth may be estimated from the calibrated VSWR data in Fig. \ref{fig:vswr_lab} and Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity}.  If we assume a usable bandwidth of [9-18] GHz based on where the HORN2-HORN8 data approaches simulation, then the fractional bandwidth is 67\%.  However, the S21 transmission begins between 5.5 and 6 GHz.  Using [6-18] GHz, the fractional bandwidth is 100\%.  Both numbers assume 18 GHz for the upper edge of the bandwidth.  The upper edge is only limited by the network analyzer bandwidth, and MEEP simulations indicate that the design radiates efficiently above 18 GHz.  Thus, both fractional bandwidths, 67\% and 100\%, should be treated as lower limits.

\subsection{Gain and Radiation Patterns}
\label{sec:rad_patterns}

Absolute realized gain, $G_{\rm r}$, was calculated S21 measurements using the three-antenna method.  The RF horns were separated by 2.05~m.  For example, $G_{r,A}=[S_{21,AB}+S_{21,AC}-S_{21,BC}+\mathrm{FSPL}]/2$, where $\mathrm{FSPL}=20\log_{10}(4\pi R/\lambda)$.  For each pair, the cable insertion loss was de-embedded from $S_{21}$.  The resulting quantity is $G_{\rm r}$ because the S21 measurement retains the impedance-mismatch loss of each antenna. The mismatch efficiency is $\eta_m=1-|S11|^2$.  Thus, the mismatch-corrected antenna gain is

\begin{align}
G&=G_r/\eta_m \\
G_{\mathrm{dBi}}&=G_{r,\mathrm{dBi}}-10\log_{10}\eta_m
\end{align}

The measured E- and H-plane radiation patterns were normalized to boresight and used to estimate the directivity $D_{\mathrm{pat}}$ from the beam solid angle. Radiation efficiency was then calculated according to $\eta_{\mathrm{rad}}=G/D_{\mathrm{pat}}$. All quantities reported in Tables~\ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and~\ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity} were grouped into 1~GHz frequency bins. For HORN1, gain values were averaged in linear power within each frequency bin before conversion to dBi. For HORN2-HORN8, the corresponding quantities were ensemble averaged over the seven antennas in linear units before conversion to logarithmic units.  The normalized radiation patterns were likewise ensemble averaged in linear power prior to calculation of $D_{\mathrm{pat}}$ and $\eta_{\mathrm{rad}}$.

The radiation pattern results are presented in Figs. \ref{fig:rad_pattern1} and \ref{fig:rad_pattern2}.  Again, the 3D printed RF horn was mounted to a tripod.  For these measurements, the 3D printed horns were connected to port 2 of the network analyzer via the RG214 N-type coaxial cable and an SMA to N connector.  Port 2 was configured as the receiving port.  Port 1 was configured as the transmitting port, and connected to an A.H. Systems SAS-510-7 log-periodic dipole array (LPDA) via a Gore PhaseFlex coaxial cable.  The LPDA and 3D printed horn were co-polarized, and mounted to a separate tripod.  The network analyzer was calibrated and set to the bandwidth [5.5-18] GHz, where the received S21 signal was $>10$ dB above thermal backgrounds.  No RF amplifier was used in the S21 setup.  The S21 parameter was recorded at 2.5 degree intervals in the E-plane and H-plane for HORN1.  The different angles were arranged by adjusting the tripod mounting mechanisms, and measured with an electronic level.  Once the technique was established, the S21 was measured at 10 degree intervals for the E-plane and H-plane for HORN1-HORN8.  For each angle and horn, the recorded S21 trace was an average of 999 measured traces (2.5\% smoothing).

For the H-plane graphs, the angle being plotted is the zenith angle in the xz-plane, and the boresight corresponds to 90 degrees.  The radiation patterns in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern1} are normalized to the S21 power at boresight, so the largest power is 0 dB in each plot.

The MEEP calculation matches the main lobe precisely in both cases, E and H-plane.  For the MEEP model, it was sufficient to treat the Electrifi material with \texttt{mp.metal} (ideal conductivity).  Treating it as a material with finite conductivity made little difference because the radiation patterns are normalized.  xxx MEEP correctly predicts that the width of the pattern is narrower in the E-plane compared to the H-plane.  Side lobes are visible in the patterns at $\pm 45$ degrees in the E-plane.  Side lobes with relative powers of -15 dB are common in RF horn designs.  MEEP correctly predicts the location of the side lobes, and comes close to modeling the side-lobe-level.  MEEP also correctly characterizes the back lobe levels in the E and H-plane, which are also below -15 dB.  Side lobes and back lobes are caused by radiation that diffracts around the edge of the RF horn opening.

Figure \ref{fig:rad_pattern2} contains the average data taken from HORN1-HORN8.  The S21 power was disaggregated by frequency, and separate radiation patterns are graphed for separate frequencies, but averaged across RF horns.  The frequencies were binned in 1 GHz bins, and the first bin center is at 6.3 GHz, so the lower edge of that bin is 5.8 GHz.  Below this frequency, noise started to interfere with our S21 measurements.  Thus, the frequency bins centers were set to 6.3, 7.3, ..., and 17.3 GHz.  The average E-plane radiation pattern at 6.3 GHz is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern2} (top left), while (top right) shows the same at 12.3 GHz.  The H-plane data at 6.3 GHz is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern2} (bottom left), while (bottom right) shows the same at 12.3 GHz.  The same conventions from Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern1} are used in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern2}.

The MEEP calculation matches the main lobes, and predicts some side lobe and back lobe behavior.  The match becomes more challenging at high frequencies when higher-order modes begin to fit within the horn body.  At low frequencies, the side-lobe-levels and back lobe levels and locations are well modeled.  At high frequencies, we measured larger back lobes than those predicted by MEEP.  However, this is likely due to leaked power in our measured data.  At high frequencies, we observe power captured by the N-to-SMA connector and cable, even without the 3D printed antennas attached.  This leaked power level is small, but measurable at the -15 dB level.  Apparently, it does not occur in every SMA-RF horn connection, because it was not observed in the data in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern1}.

An interesting facet of RF antenna fabrication arose after 3D printing our units.  The raw units finished with spurs and droplets of extra filament that acted as miniature antennas.  These surface imperfections decreased the initial S21 power.  After removing them with files and sandpaper, the inner surfaces of the horns were smoother.  The results for HORN4 are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern3}.  The data in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern3} are binned in 1 GHz bins, centered at 6, 7, ..., and 18 GHz.  The result was an increase in S21 power that varies between 2 and 10 dB, and the largest gain is found near 10 GHz.  It is difficult to explain the subtle variation in S21 gain due to smoothing and filing, because the imperfections varied in size.  However, we learned that smoothing is an important step in the fabrication process.

\subsection{Cross-Polarization Ratios}
\label{sec:results_3}

The cross-polarization results are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:7}.  The cross-polarization ratio was measured with two 3D printed horns in the same S21 configuration as the radiation pattern measurements.  The cross-polarization angle was measured with an electronic level.  The prediction for the dependence of received power versus cross-polarization angle is known from optics and antenna theory.  Let $P_{\rm r}$ be the received power, $P_{\rm t}$ be the transmitted power, and $\theta$ be the cross-polarization angle.  These are related by

\begin{equation}
P_{\rm r} = P_{\rm t} \cos^2\theta \label{eq:4}
\end{equation}

\noindent
A good reference for Eq. \ref{eq:4} may be found in the section on polarization mismatch in \cite{balanis}. When normalized, the ratio $P_{\rm r}/P_{\rm t}$ in dB is equivalent to normalized S21.  The results shown in Fig. \ref{fig:7} (left) represent linear S21 versus $\theta$, binned into 1 GHz frequency bins, and the data in Fig. \ref{fig:7} (right) are averaged over frequency.  In general, both graphs show good agreement with Eq. \ref{eq:4}.  As the cross-polarization angle approaches 90 degrees, the prediction from Eq. \ref{eq:4} tends to 0, or negative infinity in dB.  At $\theta = 90$ degrees, some high frequency data points lie between the equivalent of -20 dB and -10 dB (0.01 and 0.1), while the low-frequency data points are closer to -30 dB (0.001).  However, the average result in Fig. \ref{fig:7} (right) at $\theta = 90$ degrees lies exactly at -20 dB (0.01).

\section{Conclusions and Next Steps}
\label{sec:conc}

This work establishes an open-source design and fabrication process joining parametric design, open-source CAD, CEM with MEEP, and conductive 3D printing via Electrifi filament and Prusa XL printers.  Our process eliminates the need for electroplating or coating, and the need for expensive, proprietary modeling software.  The VSWR, radiation pattern, and cross-polarization data consistently match expectations and, in most cases, are well-modeled by MEEP.  Our fabricated designs have measured bandwidth of at least [4-18] GHz, with fractional bandwidth $>127\%$.

The next step in the research is to assemble the RF horns into a phased array.  Using a 1-to-8 RF power splitter, and programmable phase shifters, we plan to show that the collective beam from $1\times 8$ and $2\times 4$ configurations is steerable and broadband.  Thus, we have taken a significant step towards producing the first completely 3D printed, RF broadband phased array.  Once we have shown that the phased array is steerable, we plan to investigate the broadband capabilities of the design.  For example, broadband phased arrays can produce grating lobes that can be steered independently by sweeping the frequency across a wide range.  Coincidence between a low-frequency beam steered with phase shifts, and a high-frequency beam steered with a sweeping frequency, could represent an interesting way to improve sensing capability.
