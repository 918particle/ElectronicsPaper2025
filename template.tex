\Title{Broadband RF Design with MEEP: A Set of 3D-Printed Open-Source RF Horns in the multi-GHz Bandwidth}
\TitleCitation{Broadband RF Design with MEEP: A Set of 3D-Printed Open-Source RF Horns in the multi-GHz Bandwidth}

\abstract{Radio-frequency (RF) antenna design and fabrication traditionally rely on proprietary software and conventional metal machining. We present an open-source alternative that combines parametric design, open-source CAD, computational electromagnetics (CEM) using the MIT Electromagnetic Equation Propagator (MEEP), and 3D printing with conductive Electrifi filament. Using this workflow, we fabricated and characterized eight broadband RF horn antennas without electroplating or conductive coatings. Measurements of standard RF antenna properties were compared with MEEP calculations and theoretical expectations. The measured main-lobe radiation patterns are reproduced well by MEEP, and horn-to-horn cross-polarization measurements closely follow theoretical expectations. Appreciable realized gain and well-defined radiation patterns extend over approximately [6-18] GHz, corresponding to a fractional bandwidth of 100\%.  The upper frequency limit is set by the network analyzer, making the fractional bandwidth estimate a lower limit. Measurements also show that surface finishing and conductive-material properties affect system performance. These results demonstrate an open-source process for 3D-printing broadband RF phased-array systems.}

\keyword{Computational Electromagnetism (CEM), Additive Manufacturing, MEEP, \\ RF Engineering, Open-Source Design}

\section{Introduction}

Broadband RF antennas are ubiquitous tools within scientific instrumentation and communications.  Traditionally, these are designed with expensive, proprietary software packages \cite{hfss,xfdtd}.  Designs are fabricated by cutting metal with precision machine tools (subtractive manufacturing).  Compared to additive manufacturing techniques, subtractive manufacturing can be costly for rapid prototyping, and additive manufacturing relative costs improve as antenna frequency increases  \cite{segoviaguerrero2025additive-29b,alkaraki2025lowcost-d32}.  Open-source additive manufacturing boosts cost efficiency in both the design and fabrication, provided the 3D printing filament has sufficient conductivity.  At higher frequencies, traditional metallic antenna fabrication is limited by cost as dimensional tolerances become smaller. In contrast, the cost of 3D-printed antennas decreases with increasing frequency, so 3D-printing eventually becomes the preferred option \cite{alkaraki2020mmwave-bcd}.

Development of new RF antenna designs requires exploration of conductor shapes that set the boundary conditions for radiated signals.  Developing new shapes that meet design requirements has been aided by machine learning (ML) strategies \cite{gajbhiye2025comprehensive-fdb,goudos2016evolutionary-cc7,linden1999evolving-8c2}.  While mixing new ML algorithms into open CEM code is straightforward, incorporating them into proprietary software is often challenging.  Ideally, RF engineers would keep the  functionality of proprietary software, while retaining the flexibility to incorporate new ML techniques.  Creating a design process based on open CEM tools achieves this goal.

Previously, J. C. Hanson (2021) demonstrated that the open-source MEEP software package may be used as an RF antenna design tool \cite{hanson2021broadband-cab}.  MEEP operates via the FDTD algorithm for Maxwell's Equations on a Yee lattice \cite{10.1016/j.cpc.2009.11.008}.  The key to the technique is that MEEP is written to account for the scale-invariance of Maxwell's Equations.  MEEP is normally used for designs with wavelengths at the $\mu$m scale.  Designs with cm wavelengths may be created by reinterpreting length units in MEEP.  Using this approach, J. C. Hanson produced RF phased array designs in two and three dimensions using MEEP functionality \cite{hanson2021broadband-cab}.  Further, J. C. Hanson demonstrated that open-source CAD software may be used to create complex designs that can be 3D printed \cite{Hanson2022MeepCon}.

In recent years, RF antennas have begun to be fabricated with 3D printing.  The authors of \cite{alkaraki2020mmwave-bcd} presented a hybrid strategy that combined traditional PCB fabrication, non-conductive 3D printing, and metallization of 3D printed structures.  The authors of \cite{yoo2022hollow-9a5} produced a waveguide-fed holographic beam-forming antenna using a dual print-head 3D printing system that combined non-conductive PLA supports with conductive filament.  The result was a steerable beam at frequencies near 10 GHz.  The authors of \cite{yurduseven2017computational-82f} produced a Mills-Cross, frequency-diverse, metasurface antenna with 41\% fractional bandwidth, capable of electronic steering in the K-band, using a mixture of non-conductive PLA and conductive filament.  The authors of \cite{alkaraki2025lowcost-d32} fabricated three RF horns using non-conductive 3D printer filament, and painted them with conductive paint.  The resulting standard gain horns had fractional bandwidths of 42\%, 40\%, and 37.8\% after coating 3D printed polymer with silver paint.  Finally, using a similar technique the authors of \cite{betancourt2017additive-b05} managed to achieve a 140\% fractional bandwidth.

In this work, which follows the procedure proposed in \cite{Hanson2022MeepCon}, we present the first open-source, broadband, 3D-printed set of RF horns designed with MEEP and fabricated in a single step from conductive 3D-printer filament for approximately \$200-\$250 USD per horn.  Note that the authors are MEEP users, but not MEEP developers.  MEEP developers have published a comprehensive review \cite{10.1016/j.cpc.2009.11.008}.  Note also that MEEP is but one of several open-source CEM software packages that may be used to accomplish this work.  A helpful review of open-source CEM solutions, including openEMS, is found in \cite{fedeli2019open-source-8bb}. In Sec. \ref{sec:mat_meth}, we explain the parametric design, CAD, and fabrication techniques.

In Sec. \ref{sec:results}, we show that the VSWR, radiation patterns, and the cross-polarization ratios match predictions.  In Sec. \ref{sec:conc}, we discuss the limitations of our analysis, summarize key results, and discuss applications and future directions for this research.

\section{Methods and Materials}
\label{sec:mat_meth}

Figure \ref{fig:1} contains a diagram of the design and fabrication process.  Using parametric CAD design, we can specify antenna shapes using analytic functions that are then translated into the open CAD design tool kLayout.  Antenna designs created with kLayout can be imported into MEEP via the GDSII file format.  GDSII files can also be translated into 3D printer files, ensuring that the device and model match.  In Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left), the CAD design in kLayout is shown.  In Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (middle) and (right), the model is rendered using Mayavi.

Our process requires conductive 3D printer filament.  Multi3D\footnote{See \url{https://www.multi3dmaterials.com}} has produced Electrifi 3D printer filament with resistivities of a few m$\Omega$ cm, depending on the printing pattern.  Using a tungsten-tipped 3D printing head and Electrifi filament, we can complete antenna prints with volumes of $\approx 350$ cm$^3$ in several hours.  After printing, the devices are fitted with RF connectors and mounting screws.  For all measurements, the devices are mounted to custom tripods.  An Agilent Technologies E5063A vector network analyzer with bandwidth [0-18] GHz, and a 6~ft Coleman Cable 8421F M17/75-RG214 N-type coaxial cable with SMA-to-N adapter was used to measure the complex S11 parameter.  For the complex S21 and cross-polarized transmission measurements, two RG214 cables and two SMA-to-N adapters were required.  For the S21 and cross-polarization measurements, the fabricated RF horns served as both transmitters and receivers.

\subsection{CEM Design and Open-Source CAD}
\label{sec:mat_meth_1}

The open-source CEM design begins with choosing parametric design parameters for the exponential RF horn and importing them into kLayout (Fig. \ref{fig:1}-\ref{fig:1a}).  Let the origin refer to the center of the left edge of the cavity in Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left).  The horn cavity is the rectangular volume where the coaxial cable attaches, with xy-dimensions $c_l \times c_w$  (Tab. \ref{tab:1}).  The horn surfaces are the curved structures that connect the cavity to open space, with overall length $s_l$ in the x-direction, including the cavity.  The opening is the area where the surfaces stop and radiation exits the horn.  The opening has width $w$ and height $z$ in the y and z-directions, respectively.  Using these variables, Equations \ref{eq:1} and \ref{eq:2} specify the shape of the RF horn surfaces in the $xy$-plane:

\begin{align}
f(x) &= \frac{c_w}{2}\exp(k(x-c_l)) \label{eq:1} \\
k &= (s_l-c_l)^{-1} \ln\left(\frac{w}{c_w}\right) \label{eq:2}
\end{align}

\noindent
The function $f(x)$ in Equation \ref{eq:1} describes the upper surface in Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left), while $-f(x)$ describes the lower surface.  Table \ref{tab:1} contains the values for $c_l$, $c_w$, $s_l$, and $w$.  The RF horn is designed to be linearly polarized in the $y$-direction.  The RF horn design has a height $z$, out of the page in Fig. \ref{fig:1a} (left), and each conductive and dielectric surface, including the coaxial cable, has a specified thickness in the xy-plane and z-direction.

In previous studies \cite{hanson2021broadband-cab}, antenna structures were instantiated directly as native MEEP objects.  In the present workflow, the antenna geometry is instead generated parametrically and transferred to kLayout, where it can be inspected and exported in GDSII format.  The same geometry is then used for the MEEP calculation and for conversion to a printable 3D model.  This approach preserves the flexibility of a scripted parametric design while avoiding the need to reproduce a complex antenna from many simple MEEP primitives.  The implementation code used to generate and simulate the antenna is maintained in the open-source project repository cited in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsubsection{VSWR Calculations}

The voltage standing wave ratio (VSWR) is expressed in terms of the magnitude of the complex reflection coefficient $\Gamma$ at the antenna input port \cite{pozar}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{VSWR}=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}.
\label{eq:3}
\end{equation}
In the MEEP model, a broadband excitation is launched through the modeled coaxial feed.  A MEEP \texttt{FluxMonitor} is used to separate the incident and reflected contributions at the input line, from which $\Gamma$ and the corresponding VSWR are calculated\footnote{See \url{https://meep.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Python_Tutorials/GDS_Import}.}.  The model is fully three-dimensional and uses the material properties of the Electrifi filament.  Implementation details and source code are provided in the open-source repository cited in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsubsection{Feed Model}

The feed in the MEEP VSWR model follows the dimensions of the Pasternack PE4099 SMA connector.  The coaxial cable is connnected to the the horn cavity with a MEEP \texttt{mp.metal} (ideal conductivity) cylindrical center pin 1.27 mm in diameter, surrounded by a dielectric cylinder 4.11 mm in diameter with $\varepsilon_r = 2.0$ and \texttt{D\_conductivity} $= 0.01$ in MEEP units.  The outer conductor is modeled as \texttt{mp.metal}, and the far end of the cable is closed with a metal cap.  These dimensions approximate the RF cables we used in our measurements.  To model reflections that characterize S11, a broadband pulse is launched 37.6 cm from the horn cavity, approximating the coaxial TEM mode.  For normalized radiation pattern calculations, a continuous wave RF source is placed inside the cavity and the cable model is not used.  The MEEP settings are summarized in Tab. \ref{tab:meep_settings}.

\subsubsection{Radiation Pattern Calculations}

We compute the antenna radiation pattern in MEEP using near-to-far field projection\footnote{See \url{https://meep.readthedocs.io/en/master/Python_Tutorials/Near_to_Far_Field_Spectra}.}.  A \verb+NearToFarRegion+ encloses the radiating structure, and the \verb+meep.get_farfield()+ routine is used to evaluate the complex electric and magnetic fields in the far field.  In the coordinate system of Fig. \ref{fig:1a}, the horn boresight is the $+x$ direction, the $xy$-plane is the $\mathbf{E}$-plane, and the $xz$-plane is the $\mathbf{H}$-plane.

The time-averaged Poynting vector is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}^{*}\right),
\end{equation}
and the radiated power used for the angular pattern is the component projected along the radial direction,
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm r}=\mathbf{S}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}.
\end{equation}
For the $\mathbf{E}$-plane, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)$, while for the $\mathbf{H}$-plane, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}=(\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)$.  The normalized radiation pattern is reported in decibels as
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm norm}=10\log_{10}\left[\frac{P_{\rm r}}{\max(P_{\rm r})}\right] \label{eq:Pnorm}
\end{equation}
In Eq. \ref{eq:Pnorm}, $P_{\rm norm}$ and $P_{\rm r}$ are functions of $\phi$ and $\theta$ when calculating the E and H-planes, respectively. The corresponding source code is maintained in the open-source repository cited in the Data Availability Statement.

\subsection{Fabrication Technique}
\label{sec:mat_meth_2}

The parametric horn geometry was converted from the kLayout/GDSII design into a printable 3D model and fine-tuned for fabrication.  The antennas were printed directly from Multi3D Electrifi conductive filament on a Prusa XL equipped with a tungsten-tipped nozzle.  Electrifi was printed at approximately 135--145$^{\circ}$C without bed heating.  Because the horn is tall and narrow, masking tape with adhesive and removable buttresses were used to improve build-plate adhesion and mechanical stability during printing.  After printing, the temporary supports were removed and an SMA connector was attached to the cavity so that the center conductor extended into the feed region.  No secondary electroplating or conductive coating was applied to the radiating structure.  One example of the RF horns is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:3}, with a 12-inch ruler for scale.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Using the techniques described in Sec. \ref{sec:mat_meth_2}, we fabricated eight RF horns, labeled HORN1-HORN8.  All RF horns are 16.5 cm long (see ruler in Fig. \ref{fig:3}, and numbers in Tab. \ref{tab:1}).  The dimensions of the opening are $8.5 \times 2.5$ cm$^2$.  The SMA connector is mounted to the cavity, with a coaxial pin extending into the chamber such that the radiation is linearly polarized in the y-direction (see Sec. \ref{sec:mat_meth_1}).  Lateral extensions were added to the cavity so the SMA connector and mounting screw could be installed.

The network analyzer, coaxial cables, and connectors were used to measure S11, S21, and cross-opolarization transmission.  The S11 calibration plane was between the coaxial cable and the 3D printed horns, and total cable losses in the S21 measurement were also recorded.  HORN1 was manufactured first, with a 10\% larger $c_l$ parameter and Electrifi filament with conductivity of 28,600 S m$^{-1}$.  HORN2-HORN8 have the dimensions of Tab. \ref{tab:1}, and an Electrifi conductivity of 16,700 S m$^{-1}$.  Thus, the data from HORN1 and HORN2-HORN8 were analyzed separately.  The results are summarized in Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity}.

\subsection{VSWR Results}
\label{sec:vswr}

The 3D-printed RF horn was mounted to a 1.0 meter tripod with a set screw, and aimed vertically. Each antenna was connected to port~1 of the network analyzer via the RF cables. The network analyzer was calibrated over the full measurement bandwidth using a calibration kit consisting of short, open, and $50~\Omega$ load standards.  The calibration reference plane was between the RF horns and the RG214 cable so that cable losses were removed from the S11 measurement. S11 data was averaged using 999 traces per average with 2.5\% smoothing.  The calibrated S11 trace was recorded separately for each horn.  The VSWR was calculated using Eq. \ref{eq:3}, with $\Gamma=S_{11}$. 

Our MEEP/CAD matched the dimensions of the RF horns.  For the \verb+mp.Medium+ that specified material properties of the Electrifi, conductivities of 28,600 S m$^{-1}$ and 16,700 S m$^{-1}$ were assumed for HORN1 and HORN2-HORN8, respectively.  These numbers were taken from the technical specifications for the batches of Electrifi corresponding to the fabricated horns.  The insertion loss of the coaxial cable was modeled in the MEEP dielectric with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r=2.0$ and a loss tangent of $8\times 10^{-3}$ at 6 GHz.  The central conductor and exterior of the RF cable were modeled using \texttt{mp.metal} with ideal conductivity.

The simulated $\Gamma$ was calculated from two runs.  The simulation was first run with a cylindrical coaxial cable object that extended into an outer perfectly-matched layer (PML) 5 MEEP units thick.  The thick PML ensured the first run had no reflections.  The pulse passed a \texttt{FluxMonitor} MEEP object set to record flux versus frequency.  The simulation was then reset, and the RF horn object was added to the cable.  The simulation was run a second time, with $-1$ times original flux pre-loaded to the \texttt{FluxMonitor}.  The negative pre-loading step ensured that the \texttt{FluxMonitor} did not record incident flux twice.  Finally, $\Gamma$ was computed by taking the square-root of the ratio of reflected flux from the second run to the incident flux in the first run.  The MEEP results do not shift when we toggle between the two conductivities of 28,600 S m$^{-1}$ and 16,700 S m$^{-1}$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{revised_vswr_Nov19.pdf}
\caption{\label{fig:vswr_lab} Dashed line: HORN1 calibrated VSWR with $\sigma = 28,600$ S m$^{-1}$.  Solid line: HORN2-HORN8 average calibrated VSWR with $\sigma = 16,700$ S m$^{-1}$.  Dot-dashed line: MEEP simulation.}
\end{figure}

The calibrated VSWR data is shown with the calculated VSWR from MEEP in Fig. \ref{fig:vswr_lab}.  The VSWR is inversely proportional to frequency and begins to match MEEP simulation output as frequency increases.  In all cases, we observed clear S21 transmission above the noise floor starting at 5.5 GHz.  The RF signal at 5.5 GHz, however, radiated inefficiently (see Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity}).  Although HORN1 exhibits a poorer impedance match than HORN2-HORN8, the realized gain for HORN1 is larger.  The poorer impedance match for HORN1 at lower frequencies could be due to the larger HORN1 $c_l$ value.  Radiation leaving the SMA connnector pin could experience deconstructive interfence at longer wavelengths if the displacement between pin and cavity walls is not optimal.

The fact that the HORN1 realized gain is larger than the others is consistent with the relation

\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm r}=\eta_{\mathrm m}\eta_{\mathrm{rad}}D
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $\eta_{\mathrm m}=1-|\Gamma|^2$ is the mismatch efficiency, $\eta_{\mathrm{rad}}$ is the radiation efficiency, and $D$ is the directivity. A larger VSWR reduces $\eta_{\mathrm m}$, lowering the realized gain $G_{\mathrm r}$.  However, the measured directivities of HORN1 and HORN2-HORN8 consistently match (Sec. \ref{sec:rad_patterns}).  Thus, the substantially larger $G_{\mathrm r}$ observed for HORN1 indicates that the dominant difference is associated with radiation efficiency $\eta_{\mathrm rad}$ rather than impedance mismatch. This suggests that HORN1 has lower effective RF loss, potentially due to the larger conductivity.  Recall that the conductivity of HORN1 is 28,600 S m$^{-1}$, while that of HORN2-HORN8 is 16,700 S m$^{-1}$.  In the future, we propose to use the geometric specifications of HORN2-HORN8 and Electrifi filament with conductivity of at least 28,600 S m$^{-1}$.

The fractional bandwidth may be estimated from the calibrated VSWR data in Fig. \ref{fig:vswr_lab} and Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity}.  If we assume a usable bandwidth of [9-18] GHz based on where the HORN2-HORN8 data approaches simulation, then the fractional bandwidth is 67\%.  However, the S21 transmission begins between 5.5 and 6 GHz.  Using [6-18] GHz, the fractional bandwidth is 100\%.  Both numbers assume 18 GHz for the upper edge of the bandwidth.  However, that upper edge is only limited by the network analyzer bandwidth, and MEEP simulations indicate that the design radiates efficiently above 18 GHz.  Thus, both fractional bandwidths, 67\% and 100\%, should be treated as lower limits.

\subsection{Gain and Radiation Patterns}
\label{sec:rad_patterns}

The realized gain $G_{\rm r}$ was calculated from S21 measurements using the three-antenna method \cite{Balanis2016AntennaTheory}, with the transmitter and receiver separated by 2.05~m.  For example, $G_{r,A}=[S_{21,AB}+S_{21,AC}-S_{21,BC}+\mathrm{FSPL}]/2$, where $\mathrm{FSPL}=20\log_{10}(4\pi R/\lambda)$.  For each pair, the cable insertion loss was de-embedded from $S_{21}$.  The resulting quantity is $G_{\rm r}$ because the S21 measurement retains the impedance-mismatch loss of each antenna. The mismatch efficiency is $\eta_m=1-|S11|^2$.  Thus, the mismatch-corrected antenna gain is

\begin{align}
G&=G_r/\eta_m \\
G_{\mathrm{dB}}&=G_{r,\mathrm{dBi}}-10\log_{10}\eta_m
\end{align}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Nov25_plot1.pdf}
\caption{\label{fig:gain} The realized gain $G_{\mathrm r}$ versus frequency.  (Circles): results for HORN1.  (Crosses): average results for HORN2-HORN8.  (Black line): average results for HORN2-HORN8, plus 10 dB.}
\end{figure}

The $G_{\rm r}$ data in is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:gain}.  The HORN1 and HORN2-HORN8 data are separated as usual, and averaged into 0.5 GHz bins.  As is the case for most RF horn designs, $G_{\rm r}$ increases with frequency as the aperture becomes electrically larger \cite{Balanis2016AntennaTheory}.  The effect of the larger radiation efficiency $\eta_{\rm rad}$ in HORN1 is evident in Fig. \ref{fig:gain}.  The average HORN1 $G_{\rm r}$ is about 10 dB larger than that of HORN2-HORN8 across the [6-18] GHz bandwidth.

The radiation pattern results are presented in Figs.~\ref{fig:rad_pattern1} and~\ref{fig:rad_pattern2}. For these measurements, the RF horns were mounted to the tripod and connected to the receiving port of the network analyzer through the RG214 coaxial cable and an SMA-to-N connector.  An A.H. Systems SAS-510-7 log-periodic dipole array (LPDA) was connected to the transmitting port through a Gore PhaseFlex coaxial cable. The LPDA was mounted to a separate tripod, with the LPDA center 2.05 m from the co-polarized RF horn receiver.  Each antenna was 1.0 m above the lab floor.  The network analyzer was calibrated over [5.5-18] GHz, where the $S_{21}$ signal was more than 10~dB above the thermal background. No RF amplifier was used in the $S_{21}$ measurement and the network analyzer power was set to the -10 dBm maximum. For HORN1, the $S_{21}$ parameter was recorded at 2.5$^\circ$ angular intervals in both the E-plane and H-plane. The E and H-plane angles were measured with an electronic level. Once the measurement technique was established, the data were recorded for HORN1-HORN8 at 10$^\circ$ angular intervals.  Each $S_{21}$ trace was obtained from an average of 999 measured traces with 2.5\% smoothing.

The measured E and H-plane radiation patterns were normalized to boresight and used to estimate the pattern-derived directivity $D_{\mathrm{pat}}$ from the beam solid angle. The normalized radiation patterns were ensemble averaged in linear power prior to calculation of $D$. Radiation efficiency was then calculated according to $\eta_{\mathrm{rad}}=G/D$. All quantities reported in Tabs. \ref{tab:horn1_gain_directivity} and \ref{tab:horns2to8_gain_directivity} were grouped into 1 GHz frequency bins. For HORN1, gain values were averaged in linear power within each frequency bin before conversion to dBi. For HORN2-HORN8, the corresponding quantities were ensemble averaged over the seven antennas in linear units before conversion to logarithmic units. The resulting ensemble-averaged gain and directivity values were then used to calculate the reported radiation efficiencies.

The MEEP calculation matches the main lobe precisely in both the E and H-plane.  For the MEEP model, it was sufficient to treat the Electrifi material with \texttt{mp.metal} (ideal conductivity).  Including the finite conductivity of the Electrifi material makes no difference in the analysis because the radiation patterns are normalized.  Side lobes are visible in the patterns at $\pm 45$ degrees in the E-plane.  Sidelobe levels of order $-15$~dB are typical of conventional rectangular horn designs~\cite{Bull2025RHINO}.  MEEP correctly predicts the location of the side lobes, and comes close to modeling the side-lobe-level.  Our MEEP calculations also model the back lobes in the E and H-plane, which are also below -15 dB.  Sidelobes and backlobes arise in part from diffraction at the edges of the horn aperture~\cite{Yu1966HornDiffraction}.

Figure \ref{fig:rad_pattern2} contains the average data taken from HORN1-HORN8.  The S21 power was disaggregated by frequency, and separate radiation patterns are graphed for separate frequencies, but averaged across RF horns.  The frequencies were binned in 1 GHz bins, and the first bin center is at 6.3 GHz, so the lower edge of that bin is 5.8 GHz.  Below this frequency, noise started to interfere with our S21 measurements.  Thus, the frequency bins centers were set to 6.3, 7.3, ..., and 17.3 GHz.  The average E-plane radiation pattern at 6.3 GHz is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern2} (top left), while (top right) shows the same at 12.3 GHz.  The H-plane data at 6.3 GHz is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern2} (bottom left), while (bottom right) shows the same at 12.3 GHz.  The same conventions from Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern1} are used in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern2}.

Our MEEP model reproduces the measured main lobes and also captures several features of the measured sidelobe and backlobe structure. Agreement is better at the lower frequencies, where both the locations and relative levels of the sidelobes and backlobes are approximately reproduced. At higher frequencies, the measured patterns exhibit larger backlobes than those predicted by the model. The radiation pattern also becomes increasingly sensitive to the detailed horn and feed geometry as the wavelength decreases and higher-order modal structure becomes possible within the horn. Some of the excess high-frequency backlobe power may also be attributable to parasitic coupling and reflections in the measurement environment. In particular, the laboratory setup does not include an anechoic chamber, so reflected power can contribute to the measured response away from the main lobe.

An important effect emerged after the antennas were 3D printed. The horns contained small spur and droplets of excess conductive filament along the interior surfaces. These can perturb the surface-current distribution and act as unintended scattering or radiating features, thereby degrading the signal. The spurs and droplets were removed using files and sandpaper, producing smooth interior surfaces. The effect of this finishing process for one RF horn is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:rad_pattern3}. Over most of the measured band, smoothing imperfections increased the $S_{21}$ power by several dB.  The improvements approached approximately 10~dB near 10~GHz; and the effect became considerably smaller toward the upper end of the band. Because the size, number, and locations of the printing imperfections were not controlled, a detailed frequency-dependent interpretation of the improvement is difficult. Nevertheless, these measurements demonstrate that smoothing the interior surfaces is an important step.

\subsection{Cross-Polarization Ratios}
\label{sec:results_3}

The cross-polarization results are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:7}.  The cross-polarization ratio was measured with two 3D printed horns in the same S21 configuration as the radiation pattern measurements.  The cross-polarization angle was measured with an electronic level.  The prediction for the dependence of received power versus cross-polarization angle is known from optics and antenna theory.  Let $P_{\rm r}$ be the received power, $P_{\rm t}$ be the transmitted power, and $\theta$ be the cross-polarization angle.  These are related by

\begin{equation}
P_{\rm r} = P_{\rm t} \cos^2\theta \label{eq:4}
\end{equation}

\noindent
A good reference for Eq. \ref{eq:4} may be found in the section on polarization mismatch in \cite{balanis}. When normalized, the ratio $P_{\rm r}/P_{\rm t}$ in dB is equivalent to normalized S21.  The results shown in Fig. \ref{fig:7} (left) represent linear S21 versus $\theta$, binned into 1 GHz frequency bins, and the data in Fig. \ref{fig:7} (right) are averaged over frequency.  In general, both graphs show good agreement with Eq. \ref{eq:4}.  As the cross-polarization angle approaches 90 degrees, the prediction from Eq. \ref{eq:4} tends to 0, or negative infinity in dB.  At $\theta = 90$ degrees, some high frequency data points lie between the equivalent of -20 dB and -10 dB (0.01 and 0.1), while the low-frequency data points are closer to -30 dB (0.001).  However, the average result in Fig. \ref{fig:7} (right) at $\theta = 90$ degrees lies exactly at -20 dB (0.01).

\section{Conclusions and Next Steps}
\label{sec:conc}

This work establishes an open-source design and fabrication process that combines parametric antenna design, open-source CAD, computational electromagnetics using MEEP, and direct conductive 3D printing with Electrifi filament and Prusa XL printers. The process eliminates the need for post-print electroplating or conductive coatings and avoids reliance on expensive proprietary electromagnetic-simulation software. Eight functioning broadband RF horns were fabricated and characterized through measurements of VSWR, realized gain, radiation patterns, and cross-polarized transmission. The measured impedance response, and appreciable realized gain and well-defined radiation patterns extends over [6-18] GHz.  Cross-polarization measurements closely follow the expected $\cos^2\theta$ dependence throughout most of this operating range. The measured E and H-plane main lobes are also reproduced well by the MEEP calculations, with the simulations capturing the principal beam widths and several features of the sidelobe and backlobe structure.

The measurements also identify several fabrication effects that are important for directly printed conductive antennas. Surface finishing was found to have a measurable effect on transmission: smoothing excess conductive filament from the interior horn surfaces increased the measured $S_{21}$ by several dB over portions of the band. The gain measurements further demonstrate that impedance matching alone does not determine antenna performance. HORN1 exhibits substantially larger realized gain than HORN2-HORN8 despite having a poorer VSWR over portions of the band, indicating that differences in radiation efficiency are important. One likely cause is the variation in manufacturer-reported bulk conductivity of the Electrifi material. Our results show that direct conductive printing can produce broadband RF antennas with predictable properties, while also emphasizing that material characteristics and post-processing remain important details.

The next step in this research is to assemble the printed horns into a broadband phased array. Using a 1-to-8 RF power splitter and programmable phase shifters, we plan to demonstrate beam steering with both $1\times8$ and $2\times4$ array configurations. These experiments will test whether the broadband behavior demonstrated by the individual horns can be retained when the elements are incorporated into an array. Following demonstration of phase-controlled steering, we plan to investigate the frequency-dependent behavior of the array over its full operating bandwidth. In particular, wideband arrays can develop frequency-dependent grating lobes whose directions change as the operating frequency is swept. The simultaneous use of a conventional phase-steered low-frequency beam and a frequency-dependent higher-order beam may provide an additional degree of freedom for broadband sensing. Successful demonstration of these capabilities would represent a significant step toward a directly 3D-printed, broadband RF phased-array system costing approximately \$200 - \$250 USD per horn.
